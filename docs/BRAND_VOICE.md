# Passoff brand and voice

## The brand idea

**Good work deserves a clean pass.**

Passoff helps work move clearly from one person to the next: creator to reviewer, reviewer back to creator, and finished work into the world. The brand should feel like a capable teammate who brings order to a wobbly process without making a ceremony out of it.

The short brand rhythm is:

> Point. Comment. Pass it on.

## What Passoff sounds like

Passoff is plainspoken, warm, lightly whimsical, and quietly confident.

- **Plainspoken:** Say “the page” instead of “the digital surface.” Say “what happens next” instead of “workflow state.”
- **Warm:** Write for a busy person who wants to do the right thing, not for an operator who already knows the system.
- **Lightly whimsical:** Use one memorable image when it helps: “screenshot scavenger hunt,” “inbox archaeology,” or “timecode tumbleweed.”
- **Quietly confident:** Explain the outcome directly. Do not inflate ordinary features or promise magic.

## The whimsy rule

Whimsy is seasoning, not the meal. Use at most one playful phrase in a short block of copy. The sentence around it must still explain the product in everyday words.

Good:

> Each comment keeps its page and place, so your team can skip the screenshot scavenger hunt.

Too much:

> Sprinkle your thoughts on our magical feedback canvas and watch the approval fairies fly.

## Words we use

| Use | Avoid |
| --- | --- |
| Website | Web property, URL asset |
| Video | Media asset |
| Feedback | Annotation payload |
| Comment | Thread root |
| Review round | Revision cycle |
| Share a review | Generate guest access |
| Ask for approval | Initiate approval workflow |
| Ready for another look | Awaiting re-review |
| We could not load this | Fetch failed |

## Message pillars

1. **The feedback keeps its place.** Comments stay with the right page, element, video moment, and review round.
2. **Clients can start without homework.** One link, familiar words, and no required guest account.
3. **Everyone knows what happens next.** Statuses and actions describe the outcome in plain language.
4. **The final yes means something.** Approval stays attached to the version the reviewer actually saw.

## Writing checks

Before shipping a line of customer-facing copy, ask:

1. Would a first-time client understand this without a tour?
2. Does the action label say what will happen?
3. Is the playful phrase helping the idea stick, or merely showing off?
4. Is there a clear next step when something goes wrong?
5. Could any claim promise a feature the product does not yet support?
